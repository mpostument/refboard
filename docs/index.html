                How to use Refboard

                Browsing your library

                The icons down the far left are the sections, grouped by the stage of the work they are for - hover one for its name. Prepare: the Library (shows or hides the folder panel), All images and the Colour studio. Practise: Train and 3D forms. Paint: Start drawing - a timed session from the packs you ticked - and My materials. Check: open any image, a reference or a photo of your drawing, with the value tools. The dashboard has the same four stages as cards, each with what is in it. The sliders icon top right shows or hides the session panel - its tabs are Timing, Filter and Display, with the pose count and Start always at the bottom. Both panels stay the way you leave them. The 3D panel has tabs too: Object, Light, View and Use.

                Find a tool - Ctrl+K, or the magnifier at the top of the rail and in a session's toolbar: type a few letters of what you want - "loomis", "flip", "green", "perspective" - and Enter. It finds every section, every trainer and theme, and in a session every tool and guide in the toolbar, also by the other words they go by. With nothing typed, the ones you used last come first.

                The brush near the bottom of the rail is My materials: tick what you draw and paint with - watercolour, oil or gouache, bottle ink, liners, ballpoint, watercolour pencils and markers, graphite - and which one you are using now. Each gets darker in its own way, so the tools speak in its terms: the value split names its steps ("a light wash", "cross-hatching", "2B"), and the eyedropper says which step a colour is, then, for paint, how to mix it. The info drawer's Medium switches between your materials.

                The moon near the bottom of the rail picks a theme: Catppuccin Mocha (the default), Macchiato, Frappé and Latte, the original Studio dark, and Daylight - black on white, for a tablet outdoors. Edit colours... at the bottom of that menu opens the theme editor: six colours, shown live on the whole app, with how readable the text is on them; Save keeps it as your own theme, and Export and Import pass it on as a small file. A theme colours the panels and controls only: a session's stage stays black, and the picture, value scales and swatches look the same in every theme, so none of them changes how dark something reads.

                The library panel lists your packs. Clicking a pack or a folder name opens it as a grid in the middle; clicking its checkbox includes it in the pool a timed session draws from. They are separate on purpose - browsing a pack to decide whether you want it is how you end up ticking it.

                In the grid, the search box matches file, folder and pack names, and the box beside it sets the order: the folder's own (numbered poses are usually numbered for a reason), by name across folders, lightest or darkest first to pull a value study out of a pack, or shuffled - picking Shuffle again deals a new order.

                Clicking any thumbnail opens it full-screen in the same viewer a session uses - zoom, the angle tool, construction guides, the tonal-value split, the eyedropper, all of it - with ← → walking that folder in order and no clock running. Esc returns to the grid, on the image you stopped at. Draw these above the grid starts a real timed session from exactly what is on screen, search filter included.

                The grid takes the keyboard too: arrow keys move between thumbnails, Home / End jump to the first and last, Enter opens one and S skips it.

                Skip in a thumbnail's corner (or S in the viewer) marks a pose never to draw again. Skipped images stay in the grid, dimmed, so you can undo it.

                Timed sessions

                Tick one or more packs in the rail (the filter box above the list narrows it by name for a large library, without changing which packs are already ticked), then set up the right-hand panel: an interval - a preset, a custom number of seconds, or a structured warm-up/quick/long-study schedule (or build your own under Custom: any number of steps, each its own seconds-per-pose and pose count, saved for next time) - and any options: grayscale, random mirroring, a grid overlay (rule of thirds, golden ratio, diagonals, a center cross, or the classical armatures - dynamic symmetry's diagonals and reciprocals, the harmonic armature, and rabatment's squares - drawn to each image's own proportions), a ghost of the previous pose, a focal-point marker, a bell when the pose changes, fullscreen. Tone filtering (high-contrast / low-key / high-key) and skipping near-duplicates are both computed from your own library, not a fixed guess. Click Start drawing.

                During a session

                Space pause/resume · ← → previous/next pose · F flip horizontally · G grayscale · Q squint · S skip this pose for good · Esc stop. The HUD's own buttons do the same thing for a mouse.

                Your toolbar - the session toolbar holds the tools you pin; the rest are under More (the three dots), one click away. At the bottom of More, every tool is listed: a lit one is in the toolbar, and clicking it moves it to More or back. An unpinned tool keeps its key, and Ctrl+K still finds it. Reset puts back the first set.

                Workspace - w, or the sliders in the toolbar: the session's tools sorted by the question you are asking of the picture. Value - how light or dark; Colour - what colour and how to mix it; Construction - where things are and at what angle; Figure - how the body is built; My work - whether your drawing is right. Each row is the toolbar's own tool, on or off the same as from its key.

                Layers - the stack icon in the toolbar lists every overlay that can lie over the picture: construction guides, angle lines, the focal point, the ghost of the last pose, the head construction, the pose skeleton, the grid and your drawing, top to bottom as they stack. Each has a checkbox - the same as its own button or key - and its own opacity, kept for next time, so several can be on at once without drowning the picture: a faint grid under a strong skeleton. The number on the icon is how many are on. O hides them all at once without turning any off - a clean look at the picture - and O again brings them back.

                Tonal value / Notan study

                The HUD's value select (or V to cycle) posterizes the reference to 2-6 flat tones, shown side by side with the original - block in light and dark masses before chasing detail. Two tones is a pure Notan (light mass vs. dark mass); more tones reads closer to a full value scale. For a quicker check without leaving the plain original, Squint (Q) just blurs it directly - the fastest version of holding a reference at arm's length and unfocusing your eyes to see its big shapes and values.

                Understand the pose

                Open the Info drawer (I, next to the value select) for everything about the current pose rather than the session itself:

                • Scheme - swap the tonal-value view's colour: grayscale, three duotones (Van Dyke brown, Payne's grey, sepia/burnt umber), a false-colour heatmap, or outlines only (for hard and soft edges, the Edge map, x).
                • Blur - softens the reference before posterizing, so tone blocks in as soft masses instead of hard edges around every small bit of local contrast.
                • A legend showing exactly what each tone level maps to in the current scheme, a brightness histogram, and the pose's top-5 dominant colours.

                Eyedropper (E, its own HUD button) - click anywhere on the reference to read the exact hex colour and brightness percent under the cursor, and several ways to mix it from real paint - burnt sienna and ultramarine, white and phthalo blue - in parts by volume, each with the colour that mix gives and how close it lands. Medium says how you paint: in watercolour there is no white - a recipe says how strong a wash to make (from barely diluted to a pale tint), and white means leaving the paper; in oil or acrylic lighter means white paint. Paints picks the tubes they may use: a full palette, a split primary, three primaries, Zorn's four, the earths. The mixing is worked out the way paint mixes - by what each pigment absorbs across the spectrum (Kubelka-Munk, via spectral.js) - so blue and yellow make green and complements make greys, as on a real palette. Brands differ and screens differ: take it as where to start, then judge by eye. The last few samples stay listed as small swatches next to the reading - click one to copy its hex, to compare two earlier spots (skin vs. background, say) without re-aiming at either one again.

                Two more, both Options-screen checkboxes since they apply to every pose in the session rather than being switched pose to pose: Ghost previous pose overlays a faint edge-detected silhouette of whatever was on screen just before this one, to help track how a gesture or form carries from pose to pose; Focal-point marker marks this pose's own visual-weight centroid - a rough guide to where the eye tends to land first, from its actual brightness distribution rather than a guess.

                Zoom and pan

                Scroll the wheel, or use the HUD's − / 100% / + (also -/+/0 on the keyboard), to zoom into a detail - a hand, a face - up to 6x. Once zoomed in, drag anywhere to pan; double-click, or the 100% button, resets. Zoom and pan reset automatically on every new pose.

                Angle tool

                Toggle it with the HUD's Angle button (or A), then drag anywhere on the reference the way you'd hold a pencil up to check a shoulder or hip line before committing to it on paper. The first drag after turning it on also becomes a reference length - drawn faint and dashed once a second line exists - and every drag after that reads its own angle and its length as a ratio of that reference (a head height, a hand span). A few of these comparison lines stay on screen at once (the oldest quietly makes way for a new one past that), so you can measure a shoulder line and a hip line and read both together instead of one at a time. Clear lines, or turning the tool off and back on, wipes the slate for a fresh reference. High-contrast overlays (Options, or H) swaps the colour/opacity used to tell lines apart for a black-outlined white line with its own dash pattern per line - the grid overlay gets the same outline, more visible in bright light either way.

                Construction guides

                The HUD's guide select (C cycles) offers four more traditional construction aids, one at a time:

                • Vanishing point - drag (following the cursor live) to drop a point and fan guide lines out from it across the whole stage, for perspective construction on a box, a room, or a foreshortened limb.
                • Plumb line - drop a full vertical+horizontal crosshair through a point, the digital equivalent of holding a pencil straight up to check whether one landmark sits directly over another.
                • Proportion divider - drag out one unit the same way the angle tool's own reference length works, and the tool marks off repeating multiples of it automatically along the same line - "how many head-heights tall," measured instead of eyeballed.
                • Viewfinder - drag a frame over the reference: everything outside it dims, thirds divide it, and its proportions are named (4:5, 3:2...) - find a stronger picture inside the photo, cropped to the shape of your paper. The Thumbnails schedule is the exercise to use it with: eight small composition sketches.
                • Perspective check - finds the vanishing points from the picture itself. Click two points along an edge, then two along an edge parallel to it in the scene (the top and bottom of a window): where the two lines meet is a vanishing point. Four more clicks on another direction make a second, with the horizon through both and how far it is tilted; it then says what field of view that implies, and warns when the vanishing points are so close that square things will look stretched - or on the same side of the centre, which no square corner can do. A third direction finds the centre of vision, which for an uncropped photo is the middle of the frame. Works on your own drawing too - photograph it and open it with Check your own image. Backspace undoes a point.

                Only one guide is ever active at a time, and switching to a guide turns the angle tool and eyedropper off (and vice versa) - all three want the same drag gesture for different things. Selecting "Off," or switching guides, clears whatever was drawn.

                Pose skeleton

                Pose (P) finds the figure's joints with a pose model and draws over it what a teacher would: the tilt of the shoulders and hips (and whether they tilt against each other - contrapposto), the line of action in red - one curve from the head down the spine to the leg that carries the weight, the gesture to draw first - and the balance line in blue, a plumb line from the pit of the neck, which on a figure at rest lands on the foot bearing the weight. It stays on for every pose until you turn it off. The model runs in this browser; the first time, it is downloaded once (about 6 MB). The image is never sent anywhere.

                Head construction - Loomis and anime

                Head (L) draws Andrew Loomis's construction over every face in the picture, turned the way that head is turned: the cranium as a ball, its sides sliced flat into the side plane (blue - the ear sits just behind its middle), the centre line (red) over the ball and down the face, and the face in thirds (yellow) - hairline, brow, base of the nose, chin. Lines on the far side of the head are dashed. The note says how far the head is turned, tipped and tilted, and what that does to the features. Draw the construction first, then check it against this. It needs the face fairly large in the picture; like the pose model, the face model runs in this browser and is downloaded once (about 4 MB).

                Anime, at the start of the note, keeps the ball, the side plane and the centre line - how the head is turned - and draws the face as anime does (pink): the eyes lower and much bigger, one eye apart, with a heavy upper lash line and a tall iris; a small nose and mouth close under them; the jaw nearly straight to a pointed chin. Take a photo of anyone at a hard angle - from below, three-quarter, tilted - and see where anime features sit on it. The choice is kept.

                Edge map - hard and soft

                Edge map (X, or Workspace > Value) draws every edge of the picture worth painting, coloured by how hard it is: red where the change happens at once, blue where it fades over a distance. Hardness is how quick the change is, not how big - a soft edge between black and white is still soft. In watercolour, red is laid on dry paper, blue run in wet-in-wet or softened with a damp brush; the note says it in the terms of your medium (My materials), and how the hard and the soft are shared out. Its opacity is in Layers. For watercolour, How to draw it has the same map as a step.

                Build up in stages

                Tick Build up in stages under Timing for copying a master painting or a long study. Each new pose is shown first as two masses of light and dark, then as three values, then as soft masses of colour, and only in the last stretch as it really is - the order a painting is built in, so the eye cannot go to the detail first. The interval is shared between the stages; → moves to the next one early. It and memory drawing are one or the other.

                Frames from a video

                Drop a video on Check your own image (or pick one) and twenty frames are taken from it at random - one from each stretch of the clip, so they cover all of it - to draw a session from. Dancing, sport and animals give poses in real movement that no posed photo has. The frames are made in this browser; the video goes nowhere.

                Draw from memory

                Tick Draw from memory under Timing and every new pose is shown only for the study time, then covered. The interval is your drawing time; when it runs out - or you click the cover, or press → - the reference comes back and the clock stops, so you can check. D lays a photo of your drawing over it, the same as Compare below. Previous shows an earlier pose plainly.

                Compare your drawing

                Compare (D) lays a photo or scan of what you drew over the reference - pick the file, drop it on the stage, or paste it. The clock pauses. Lines in red lifts just your marks off the paper (an unevenly lit phone photo of grey paper is fine) and draws them in red over the reference; See-through photo and Blink are the other two ways to look. Drag to move it, Shift+scroll to size it, Alt+scroll to turn it. Line up 2 points is the quick way: click two landmarks on the reference (top of the head, a heel), then the same two on your drawing, and it is sized, turned and moved so they meet - what is left between them is your proportions and angles as drawn. D hides it for a moment; it goes away with the pose, and never leaves this browser.

                Colour studio

                Drop, paste or choose any image - a reference, a master painting, a painting of your own. On the wheel, hue runs round and chroma (how intense a colour is) out from grey in the middle; every pixel of the image is a dot on it, so you see at once which hues it lives in and how grey it really is. The numbered circles are its palette, found from its pixels and sized by how much of the picture each covers; the list beside gives each one with its value as grey, and warns where two different colours have the same value - they will merge in a black-and-white study. Value shows the whole image that way.

                A gamut mask (James Gurney's tool) is a shape on the wheel that limits which colours a painting may use. Pick one, drag inside it to turn it to other hues, drag a corner to reshape it, and In gamut repaints the image with only the colours inside it, its values untouched - try a palette on a picture before committing paint to it.

                Paints chooses a palette of real tubes - the same choice as the eyedropper's. The dashed outline on the wheel is every colour those paints can mix, and the note says how much of the picture lies inside it: Zorn's four reach flesh and warm greys but no true blue or green, so a sky has to become a cool grey - which is the point of a limited palette. Under each palette colour is how to mix it from them; click it for other ways. Point at a palette colour and the picture shows only where it is used, the rest dimmed; click to keep it that way. The image never leaves this browser.

                Your practice

                The dashboard counts what you have actually drawn: poses, time, sessions, and the run of consecutive days you are currently on - a day you have not started yet does not break it. The calendar under them is the last thirteen weeks, one square per day, brighter for a heavier day, measured against your own busiest one rather than a fixed number. Hover a square for the date and count.

                Only timed sessions count. Browsing the grid is looking, not drawing, and is deliberately never logged. The most recent 50 sessions are kept - enough for months of practice - and they sync between your devices along with the rest of the setup where that is configured.

                Check your own image

                The drop zone on the setup screen works with no library at all - it's the whole of what runs on the live demo. Drop one image to view it full-screen with every tool above, paused with no timer running; drop several to run a real timed session against them. Nothing is ever uploaded anywhere - it stays local to this browser.

                3D forms

                Simple forms - cube, sphere, cylinder, cone, pyramid, prism, torus, capsule, egg, or a model of your own - under light you control: the forms every shading lesson starts with, where light, halftone, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow are easiest to tell apart. Needs no library; the 3D engine (three.js) is fetched from a CDN the first time you open the view.

                Forms: up to six on the floor at once - Add form, pick one by its number (in the panel, or the badge over it in the view), and its shape, surface, placement, proportions and rotation are what the panel then edits. Forms cast shadows on each other. Load model... (or drop the file on the view) takes a .glb, self-contained .gltf or .obj - a mannequin, a planes-of- the-head bust, a skull - and treats it like any other form. The Figure shape is a jointed wooden mannequin: click a dot on a joint (or a part of it) to pick it, then drag its rings, press R, or use the Bend / Twist / Lean sliders under Pose; click the picked dot again for the whole figure. Pose has the classic poses, Random pose, Mirror and Reset; Alt+R straightens one joint. It stands on whatever part of it is lowest, so a kneeling figure kneels. A model file stays in the browser, and only until the page is reloaded. Contour lines on a model need texture coordinates in the file.

                Head is a real head - a 3D scan - and Head planes is the same head cut into a few hundred flat facets, the way the Asaro planes head is: each turn of the form is one plane and one value, so it shows where the light turns into shadow on a face and why. The scan is fetched the first time you pick a head (about 400 KB, once); until then, or offline, a simpler sculpted head stands in. Contour lines on both run round the head and up it, like calliper lines on a sculpture. Head scan: Lee Perry-Smith, Infinite-Realities (CC BY 3.0), via the three.js examples. Hand poses like the figure, joint by joint - the wrist, three joints on each finger and the thumb - with its own poses: relaxed, spread, fist, pointing, pinching, gripping. In Draw random forms a head nods, tilts and turns and a hand takes a new pose, instead of being swapped for a random shape.

                Anime head is built the anime way - the same proportions as the Anime style of Head construction on a photo: a big round cranium, the jaw almost straight to a pointed chin, a nose that is barely there, and the eyes drawn on - the heavy upper lash line, the tall iris darker under it, the gleam on the side the light comes from. Its face is a flat mask, not the front of a ball, which is why anime eyes do not turn the way real ones do: the note under the view gives the far eye's width against the near one's at the angle you see it from, and what it would be on a round head, with what to watch at that angle. With a ComfyUI, Draw it at this angle asks Generate for anime heads from the same side, to hold against the 3D one. The shapes are listed in kinds: forms, figure and head, cloth, your models.

                Its Hair (Object tab, while the anime head is selected) is in locks, as anime draws it: a few pointed ribbons with some thickness over a mass that hides the scalp, each lying on the skull and then hanging from the widest point it passed - Short, Bob or Long, or none. Its light is the ring anime paints: a hair is a line, and a line shines where it lies square to the light, so the shine is a band round the head, broken where each lock turns - turn the head or move the light and it slides. On an Anime head it is cel shaded, and the fringe throws a hard shadow of its own shape on the forehead. The colours are named as Generate names them; your character sheets add their characters' hair colours, and the form's own colour is its Skin. Draw it at this angle sends the hair along.

                Cloth over a ball, Hanging cloth and Curtain are drapery, each one of the fold types drawing books teach: drop folds falling from what holds the cloth up, diaper folds sagging between two points, pipe folds running down from a gathered edge. The cloth is simulated falling the first time you pick one - a moment's wait, once - and lit and shaded like every other form. Contour lines follow the weave, which is the easiest way to see how a fold turns.

                Move, turn and stretch a form in the view, the way Blender does it. The selected form carries handles - Move, Rotate or Scale, picked top-left of the view (pressing the lit one hides them): drag an arrow, a ring or a square along its axis, the middle square to slide anywhere on the floor, the white circle to turn about your line of sight or scale evenly. Or, with the pointer over the view, press G, R or S and move the mouse; then X, Y or Z to lock an axis (twice for the form's own axis while turning), type a number for an exact amount, hold Ctrl to snap, and click or Enter to keep it - right-click or Esc puts it back. Z is up and Y into the picture, as in Blender; moving on Z lifts a form off the floor (never through it), as does Lift under Placement. Click a form to select it - a click on the one already selected lights that spot, as before. Shift+D duplicates the form, Delete removes it, Alt+G / R / S resets its place, rotation or size, Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Shift+Z undo and redo any of these. The axis ball top right shows which way X, Y and Z point: click a ball to look along that axis from its side - Z from above, Y from behind - and click the one facing you to see the opposite side. The numpad's 1, 3, 7 do the same for front, side and top, and 4 6 8 2 orbit in 15° steps. The keys work on any keyboard layout.

                Surface: Matte (plaster - no highlight), Satin (a broad soft highlight), Glossy (a small sharp highlight on top of the colour), Metal (mirrors its surroundings), Glass (see-through; its cast shadow stays solid here) and Velvet (brightest at the edges - the reverse of matte). Shine fine-tunes any of them.

                Drag to orbit and scroll or pinch to zoom. The lights stay where they are while you walk around the forms, the way lamps in a studio do - the Direction sliders update to say where they now are relative to you. Reset view under Camera puts the camera back.

                Aim the light in the view itself: click the selected form to make that spot the brightest one; click the floor to throw the shadow there (a far click is a low, long shadow). The yellow handle is the light: drag it and a lamp follows the pointer - pulling it away from the forms moves it further off, and its "lamp ×" number changes as it goes; scroll over it - or Shift+scroll anywhere - to move it nearer or further without moving it sideways. The number is its distance in multiples of the scene's radius. All the way out it becomes the sun: parallel rays, a shadow the width of the form. Close in, a lamp: the side nearer it visibly brighter than the far side, and a shadow that fans out. The lamp stands off the form you last lit and goes with it when you move that form. A click on another form only selects it - the light stays put until you click the selected form or the floor. Brightness at the forms is kept the same either way, so what changes is the character of the light, not the exposure. A lamp out of the picture gets a hollow handle on the frame edge that works the same way.

                Second light adds a fill (soft, from the other side of the camera - lifts the shadow side) or a rim (from behind - a bright edge that separates the form from the background), shown as a small blue dot. Fill under Ambient lifts every shadow evenly; Bounce is reflected light coming up off the floor in the floor's own colour - set it to zero to see what it was doing inside the core shadow.

                Light and shadow zones paints the families straight onto the forms - highlight, light, halftone, terminator, core shadow, reflected light, cast shadow - computed from the actual light and its shadows, with a key in the corner. On the floor only the cast shadow is marked.

                Perspective: the 1-point / 2-point / 3-point buttons square the forms up and place the camera so exactly that many sets of edges converge; Lens sets how hard they converge - 18mm is steep, 200mm nearly parallel, and the forms stay the same size either way. Vanishing points draws the box around the selected form with every edge run out to where it meets, one colour per direction (a point off the picture is pinned to the frame edge). Ellipses and axis draws a round form's cross-sections and its axis - every ellipse's minor axis lies along it, the rule a leaning cylinder most often breaks. Floor grid, Eye-level line and Cross-contour lines do what they say.

                Open in viewer sends the render to the full-screen viewer with every tool above - try the tonal-value split on it. Draw random forms renders a set of random poses and lighting and starts a timed session with them, using the Interval and Schedule on the right; tick Any shape to vary the forms and their proportions too. Memory drill shows a fresh random scene for the time you set, hides it while you draw it from memory, and shows it again when you click. View drill sets a random scene and a task - draw it from the other side, from above, under another light or through another lens - and Show the answer swings the view to exactly that; Check in viewer then opens the answer where Compare can lay a photo of your drawing over it. Guides, zones and handles are never part of these renders.

                Clean (top left, or Shift+Alt+Z as in Blender) hides every handle, number, skeleton and guide, leaving just the lit scene; press it again or Esc to come back.

                Saved scenes keeps named setups in this browser; Copy link puts the whole scene in a link that opens it anywhere - on the tablet, or for someone else (your own models are not included).

                Train

                Short drills, ten tasks a round, each scored out of 100 with the answer shown and a line on why - your best and recent rounds are kept on the cards. Enter goes on, Esc goes back to the list.

                • Value - how light is the patch? Pick its step 1-9 on a grey scale, against surrounds built to fool you (simultaneous contrast, gradients, strong colour), then with coloured patches.
                • Colour - mix the target on hue, chroma and value sliders; the answer says which of the three was off and which way. The second half shows the target on a coloured surround.
                • Anatomy - click the height of a landmark on an eight-heads figure (chin, nipples, navel, crotch, fingertips, knees...) or on the head (eyes, brow, nose, mouth, hairline); the canon is drawn in after.
                • Angles and proportions - turn a line to copy an angle; say how many times one length goes into another.
                • Lines and ellipses - one stroke each: a straight line between two dots, a circle, an ellipse of a given degree. Scored on how far the stroke strays, with the true shape drawn over it.
                • Perspective - click where a box face's edges vanish, or where eye level runs across a box in two-point perspective.
                • Gesture - a random pose on the 3D figure for 30 s, 1 or 2 minutes, then hidden while you finish; mark yourself against it, or open it in the viewer and Compare a photo of your drawing.
                • Negative space - the empty shape by the dot: pick it out of three, the other two the same area stretched or turned.
                • Tone map - on a picture from your library (or a render): which of two spots is darker, where the darkest dark and lightest light are, judged on the picture squinted.
                • Colour temperature - which of two is warmer or cooler; warmer means leaning nearer orange, whatever the value or intensity.
                • Edges - the hardest or softest edge of four, with contrast set to fool you.
                • Ellipses and centres - turn a cylinder's end ellipse to fit its axis; click the true middle of a rectangle in perspective.

                Your uploads and your data

                Everything you give the app is kept: pictures and videos dropped on Check your own image, photos of your drawing in Compare, the Colour studio's pictures. They are listed under the drop zone as Your uploads - click one to open it again in the workspace (a video gives a new set of frames), or the cross on it to forget it. The line above the list says plainly where they are kept.

                Behind the container they are saved on its disk, with your library, and join it as the Uploads pack - tick it and draw from them like any pack. On the web page there is no server: they last until the page is reloaded, unless you tick Keep in this browser, which keeps them in this browser on this device.

                Your data... beside the list (or Ctrl+K, "backup") makes a backup: one .zip with your settings, materials, themes, 3D scenes, practice log, trainer scores and every upload. Restore from a backup reads one, says what is in it and asks before it puts it back. Keep one somewhere safe, or use it to move to another computer - from the web page to the container or back.

                What's new - after an update, a short list of what came with it, each with a button to try it. Again from the footer, Help or Ctrl+K.

                Rescan library

                Shown on the setup screen once a library index has loaded. Click it to ask the container to reindex right away instead of waiting for its next scheduled pass - useful right after dropping a new pack into the mounted folder.
